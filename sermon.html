📖 Sermon Preparation Guide

Follow these steps from prayer and preparation through reviewing your finished sermon.

Lesson 1 of 18

1. Pray and Begin With the Right Purpose

Begin by asking God for wisdom, understanding, humility, and faithfulness to Scripture.

Ask yourself: Am I preparing this sermon to faithfully teach God's Word and help people understand and apply it?

2. Choose the Sermon Topic or Passage

Choose a biblical passage or a clear biblical topic. Avoid starting with an idea and forcing Scripture to support it.

Suggestion: Start with the Scripture whenever possible. Let the text help determine the message.

3. Read the Passage Carefully

Read the passage several times. Read what comes before and after it. Look for repeated words, commands, promises, warnings, contrasts, and important ideas.

Question: What does the passage actually say?

4. Study the Context

Determine who wrote the passage, who received it, what was happening, and how the passage fits into the surrounding chapter and book.

Why it matters: Context helps prevent taking a verse out of its intended meaning.

5. Find the Main Biblical Truth

Ask what central truth the passage teaches.

Try this: Write the main truth in one clear sentence.

6. Study Important Hebrew or Greek Words

Use the Hebrew and Greek tools when a word's original meaning helps clarify the passage.

Important: Original-language study should clarify Scripture, not replace the context of Scripture.

7. Use the Bible Dictionary

Look up important people, places, concepts, customs, and theological terms connected to your passage.

Question: Is there background information that would help someone understand this passage?

8. Use the Concordance

Search important words and see how Scripture uses those words elsewhere.

Suggestion: Compare related passages rather than relying on one verse alone.

9. Compare Bible Translations

Compare KJV, WEB, and ASV to notice wording differences that may help clarify the passage.

Question: What does every translation clearly communicate?

10. Read the Commentaries

Compare trusted commentaries and look for historical, grammatical, and theological observations.

Remember: Commentaries are study helps. Scripture remains the primary source.

11. Develop the Main Sermon Point

Turn your main biblical truth into a clear statement that your listeners can remember.

Example: "Because God has forgiven us, we should learn to forgive others."

12. Build Supporting Points

Develop two or three major points that explain and support the main biblical truth.

Tip: Every major point should connect back to the main message.

13. Explain the Scripture

Explain what the passage means before moving to personal opinions or applications.

Ask: What does this verse mean in its original context?

14. Give Practical Application

Show people how the biblical truth should affect their lives, choices, relationships, faith, and actions.

Question: What should someone do differently because of this truth?

15. Write the Introduction

Introduce the problem, question, situation, or biblical truth that will lead people into the message.

Goal: Get attention while clearly pointing toward the biblical message.

16. Write the Conclusion

Bring the sermon back to the main biblical truth. Do not introduce a completely new message at the end.

Question: What do I want people to remember after they leave?

17. Put the Sermon Together

Organize the introduction, Scripture, explanation, points, illustrations, applications, and conclusion.

Check: Does every part of the sermon support the central biblical truth?

18. Review for Biblical Accuracy and Clarity

Read through the complete sermon carefully. Check every Scripture reference and make sure your conclusions actually follow the biblical text.

Final questions: Is it biblical? Is it clear? Is it faithful to context? Is it practical? Can someone understand the main message?

🧭 Keep the Guide Beside You

As you write, keep asking:

1. What does Scripture say?
2. What does Scripture mean in context?
3. What biblical truth should people understand?
4. How should this truth change our lives?
5. Can I support my teaching with Scripture?
6. Am I explaining Scripture or merely giving my opinion?
7. Is the message clear enough for someone hearing it for the first time?
